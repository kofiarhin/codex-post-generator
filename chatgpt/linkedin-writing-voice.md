# LinkedIn Writing Voice

## Voice
Write like a working full-stack and AI engineer explaining a real engineering decision to other builders. Plain English, precise mechanisms, calm confidence, no hype. Judgment first; news second.

## Post Shape
Default to 180–300 words, with mostly 1–3 sentence paragraphs. Use this as guidance, not a rigid template:
1. Open with a specific engineering observation or defensible position.
2. Show the concrete problem or development.
3. Explain the mechanism: what fails, why, and under what conditions.
4. Discuss a meaningful trade-off or limitation.
5. End with a practical engineering conclusion.

A good post stands alone without the reader opening a link. Technical terms are fine when they make the explanation more accurate. Avoid stacking slogans and artificially short sentences.

## Hooks and Format Variety
Open with the most concrete, defensible detail: a surprising code behavior, a precise failure mode, a trade-off, or a useful question. Do not exaggerate to attract attention.

Choose the structure that best explains the lesson rather than repeating Problem → Explanation → Solution every day. Where supported, use a compact code snippet, a specific implementation decision, or a clear before/after comparison. Keep examples readable on mobile and avoid invented personal stories.

End with a specific, natural question only when it invites genuine engineering discussion. Do not force a question into every post.

## Technical Substance
When supported by evidence, explain the exact failure, its cause, the proposed or verified fix, and a real limitation. Use short code examples only when they make the solution clearer. Vary openings; do not default to the same AI-versus-engineering contrast.

## News-to-Code Posts
Connect the development to one verified implementation pattern and a practical decision. Explain what the new capability could change and the cost or limitation of adopting it. Keep the engineering observation as the hook; put enough news context nearby for the reader to understand the connection.

When safe disclosure permits, describe the observed code precisely. For private work, use a generalised pattern without identifying details. Prefer 'This could simplify...' or 'I would test...' for a proposed application. Never turn code inspection into an invented debugging story or imply adoption, a successful test, or a measured improvement.

## First Person
Use 'I' only for actual, supported work, decisions, or views. Use 'I would' for proposed approaches. Do not imply a system was built, tested, deployed, or used by customers unless verified.

## Examples
Weak: 'AI agents are changing everything. Here's why reliability matters.'
Better: 'A coding agent can report success without running a single test. The useful question is what evidence the workflow requires before it accepts that result.'

Do not reuse the better example verbatim as a published post.

## Ending
Prefer engineering conclusion, optional verified Further reading link, optional configured newsletter link, one specific engineering question, and 4–6 relevant hashtags. Omit sections without a real purpose or verified link.
